\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A deep network trained by gradient descent does not merely reach \emph{a} minimiser of its
training loss; on an over-parameterised model it reaches a particular one, and which one it
reaches is a property of the optimiser rather than of the objective. This selection effect is
usually called the \emph{implicit bias} of the optimiser, and for factored linear models it is by
now well characterised: depth drives the solution toward low rank, and the effect strengthens as
layers are added \citep{gunasekar2017implicit,arora2019implicit,li2021towards}.

Almost all of that characterisation is obtained by looking at \emph{endpoints}. One trains to
convergence, inspects the solution, and repeats the experiment with a different depth, width, or
initialisation; the optimiser's preference is then inferred from how the endpoint moves. This is
an indirect instrument, for a reason that is structural rather than practical: the comparison has
no reference point. To say that gradient descent's step is biased, one would have to say what an
unbiased step would have been, and in weight space there is no canonical answer --- the loss
supplies a gradient, and the gradient is precisely the object whose bias is in question.

This paper supplies the missing reference by changing the space in which the question is asked.
For a bias-free network built from linear maps and positively homogeneous gates, the function
computed at an input $x$ factors through a matrix,
\begin{equation}
  f(x) = P(x)\,x ,
  \label{eq:fx}
\end{equation}
where $P(x)$ is the input--output Jacobian at $x$ (defined precisely in
Section~\ref{sec:setup}). In this \emph{operator} view the loss has a direct opinion about what
should happen: it asks the operator to move along $-\partial \ell/\partial P(x)$. That request is
well defined, it does not mention the parameterisation, and every optimiser can be measured
against it.

What a weight update actually does to the operator is a different matter. The update $\Delta W$ is
applied to the factors, and the factorisation converts it into an operator change through a linear
map $M$ that depends on the current weights. Two questions then become separable, and both are
measurable:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{How much of what the loss asked for is achievable at all?} The range of $M$ may
        not contain the requested operator change. This is a property of the architecture and the
        current weights, and \emph{no} optimiser can do better than it.
  \item \textbf{Of the achievable part, how much does a given optimiser take?} Gradient descent,
        Adam, or any other rule produces some $\Delta W$; the operator change it induces can be
        compared in direction and magnitude with the best achievable one. This is a property of
        the update rule.
\end{enumerate}

Separating these two is the contribution of this paper. It requires being able to compute, at any
point in training, the weight update that realises a prescribed operator change as exactly as the
architecture permits and with the smallest possible norm --- what we call the \emph{reference
step}. We give solvers for it in two regimes: exactly, in closed form, for deep linear chains
(Section~\ref{sec:linear}), and as a least-squares problem on the step map for gated networks
(Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*.},leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{A reference step targeting the operator.} Given a prescribed change of the
        operator, we compute the minimum-norm weight update realising it, up to the reachable set.
        The construction is a preconditioned gradient step (Remark~\ref{rem:precond}) and we do not
        claim the family as new: on a deep linear network with whitened inputs it reduces exactly to
        the natural gradient of \citet{bernacchia2018exact}. What is new is the target. Prescribing
        an operator change constrains the full Jacobian at every input, where prescribing an output
        change --- as in the closest prior formulation, \citet{evans2026theory} --- constrains only
        its action along that input; the two separate precisely when the network is gated or the
        inputs are not whitened (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}, \ref{sec:nonlinear}).
  \item \textbf{A decomposition of the bias into an architectural and an algorithmic part.}
        Reachability measures what the architecture forbids; deflection measures what the
        optimiser gives up within what is allowed. The first is optimiser-independent, the second
        is scale-invariant and therefore independent of the learning rate
        (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}).
  \item \textbf{Measurements.} What the decomposition finds across model classes,
        initialisations, and optimisers (Sections~\ref{sec:linear-exp}, \ref{sec:nonlinear-exp}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Setup: factored models, the operator, and the reference step}
\label{sec:setup}

This section defines every object used later, in order of dependence. Nothing here is specific to
a particular nonlinearity: the same definitions serve the deep linear networks of
Section~\ref{sec:linear} and the gated networks of Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear}, which is what
makes the diagnostics of Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics} comparable across model classes.

\subsection{Factored models}
\label{sec:models}

Throughout, a model has $L$ weight matrices $W_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell \times n_{\ell-1}}$ for
$\ell = 1,\dots,L$, and no bias terms. We write $W = (W_1,\dots,W_L)$ for the collection and
$\Delta W = (\Delta W_1,\dots,\Delta W_L)$ for an update. Between consecutive weight matrices sits
a \emph{gate} $D_\ell$, a matrix that may depend on the input. We consider five instances.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item[Deep linear network (DLN).] $D_\ell = I$ for all $\ell$.
  \item[Fixed-gate linear network (FGLN).] $D_\ell$ is a fixed diagonal matrix, the same for every
        input. This interpolates between the linear and the gated case: the contexts below become
        input-dependent in form but not in value.
  \item[ReLU multilayer perceptron.] $D_\ell(x) = \mathrm{diag}\big(\mathbf{1}[z_\ell(x) > 0]\big)$,
        where $z_\ell(x)$ is the pre-activation at layer $\ell$.
  \item[Leaky ReLU multilayer perceptron.] $D_\ell(x) = \mathrm{diag}\big(\mathbf{1}[z_\ell(x)>0]
        + \alpha\,\mathbf{1}[z_\ell(x)\le 0]\big)$ with $\alpha = 0.1$. Every gate entry is
        strictly positive, so no unit can ever be dead. This is a control rather than a model of
        interest: it holds depth, width and the factorisation fixed while removing the possibility
        of unit death, which separates "the optimiser prunes units" from "the optimiser reduces
        how much the gate pattern depends on the input" (Section~\ref{sec:e8}). It is used where
        that distinction is at stake and omitted elsewhere.
  \item[CReLU multilayer perceptron.] The concatenated rectifier \citep{shang2016crelu} doubles the
        feature dimension,
        $c = [\,\mathrm{relu}(z)\,;\,\mathrm{relu}(-z)\,]$, which can be written $c = D(z)\,z$ with
        the rectangular gate
        \begin{equation}
          D(z) \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{1}[z>0]) \\[2pt]
                                     -\,\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{1}[z<0]) \end{bmatrix}
          \in \mathbb{R}^{2d \times d},
          \qquad D(z)^\top D(z) = I .
          \label{eq:crelu}
        \end{equation}
        The isometry $D^\top D = I$ is what makes this architecture useful to us, for reasons
        given in Section~\ref{sec:lookslinear}.
\end{description}

All five gates are positively homogeneous of degree zero in the pre-activation, so the network is
positively homogeneous of degree one and \eqref{eq:fx} holds exactly rather than approximately.
Leaky ReLU is worth checking on this point rather than assuming it: $\max(z, \alpha z)$ is
positively homogeneous of degree one for any $\alpha$, so the gate is degree zero and the
factorisation is exact there too --- which is what makes it usable as a control at all.

Not every experiment uses all five. The deep linear and fixed-gate models are where the operator is
input-independent and the geometry can be drawn exactly (Section~\ref{sec:linear}); ReLU and CReLU
carry the main comparisons; leaky ReLU appears where dead units would otherwise confound the
reading. Each experiment states which it uses.

\subsection{The operator and its contexts}
\label{sec:contexts}

\begin{definition}[Operator]
The \emph{operator} at input $x$ is
\begin{equation}
  P(x) \;=\; W_L\, D_{L-1}(x)\, W_{L-1} \cdots D_1(x)\, W_1
  \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{n_L \times n_0},
  \label{eq:P}
\end{equation}
so that $f(x) = P(x)\,x$. Where the gates are input-independent (DLN, FGLN) we write $P$ without
the argument, and $P$ is then shared by all inputs.
\end{definition}

For a gated network $P(x)$ is the Jacobian $\partial f/\partial x$ almost everywhere, so
\eqref{eq:P} is not a new object but the familiar one, written in a form that exposes how each
layer enters.

\begin{definition}[Contexts]
The \emph{right contexts} and \emph{left contexts} are defined by the recursions
\begin{equation}
  B_1 = I, \qquad B_{\ell+1} = D_\ell W_\ell B_\ell,
  \qquad\qquad
  A_L = I, \qquad A_\ell = A_{\ell+1} W_{\ell+1} D_\ell .
  \label{eq:contexts}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

They are exactly the parts of the chain to the right and to the left of a given layer, and by
construction
\begin{equation}
  P(x) \;=\; A_\ell(x)\, W_\ell\, B_\ell(x)
  \qquad \text{for every } \ell = 1,\dots,L .
  \label{eq:AWB}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:AWB} is the only structural fact we use, and it holds verbatim for all four
model classes of Section~\ref{sec:models}; only the value of $D_\ell$ changes. This is why a
single implementation and a single set of diagnostics cover the whole family.

\subsection{What the loss asks of the operator}
\label{sec:target}

Let $\ell(\cdot)$ be a per-example loss and $\mathcal{L}$ the average over a batch. Because
$f(x) = P(x)x$, the loss can be differentiated with respect to the operator directly.

\begin{definition}[Operator gradient]
$G(x) \;:=\; \partial \ell / \partial P(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_L \times n_0}$.
\end{definition}

For the two losses used in this paper $G$ is rank one:
\begin{equation}
  G(x) = \big(f(x) - y\big)\,x^\top \quad\text{(squared loss)},
  \qquad
  G(x) = \big(\mathrm{softmax}(f(x)) - e_y\big)\,x^\top \quad\text{(cross-entropy)} .
  \label{eq:G}
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{eq:AWB} with the chain rule gives the familiar backpropagated gradient in the
notation above,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W_\ell}
  \;=\; \sum_x A_\ell(x)^\top\, G(x)\, B_\ell(x)^\top .
  \label{eq:backprop}
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Target]
Given a step size $\eta > 0$, the \emph{target} is the collection of operator changes the loss
requests, one per input:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_\star \;:=\; \big(-\eta\, G(x)\big)_x .
  \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The target is defined without reference to the parameterisation. It is the statement ``move the
operator this way'', and it is the reference against which everything below is measured.

\subsection{The step map and the reference step}
\label{sec:stepmap}

An update $\Delta W$ changes the operator. To first order, substituting into \eqref{eq:P} and
collecting the terms linear in $\Delta W$,
\begin{equation}
  P\big(x;\, W + \Delta W\big) - P\big(x;\, W\big)
  \;=\; \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} A_\ell(x)\, \Delta W_\ell\, B_\ell(x) \;+\; O\!\left(\|\Delta W\|^2\right).
  \label{eq:firstorder}
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Step map]
The \emph{step map} is the linear operator sending a weight update to the collection of induced
operator changes,
\begin{equation}
  M(\Delta W) \;:=\; \Big( \textstyle\sum_{\ell} A_\ell(x)\, \Delta W_\ell\, B_\ell(x) \Big)_x ,
  \label{eq:M}
\end{equation}
with adjoint $\;(M^\top H)_\ell = \sum_x A_\ell(x)^\top H_x B_\ell(x)^\top$. We write $\Pi$ for the
orthogonal projector onto $\mathrm{range}(M)$.
\end{definition}

Comparing the adjoint with \eqref{eq:backprop} shows that gradient descent's weight step is
$\Delta W^{\mathrm{GD}} \propto M^\top \Delta_\star$. It is the adjoint applied to the target, not
the solution of any equation involving the target.

What we would like to ask for is exact: the smallest displacement whose \emph{realised} operator
change is the requested one,
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\Delta W} \; \sum_\ell \|\Delta W_\ell\|_F^2
  \quad \text{subject to} \quad
  P(x; W + \Delta W) - P(x; W) \;=\; \Delta_\star(x) \ \ \text{for every } x .
  \label{eq:exact}
\end{equation}
Note what is being minimised: the \emph{displacement} from the current weights, not the weights
themselves. Minimising $\sum_\ell\|W_\ell\|^2$ subject to realising a target is a different
problem --- the smallest factors realising it from scratch --- whose answer does not depend on
where the weights currently are, and whose classical solution is balanced. That is not the
question a training step asks.

Problem \eqref{eq:exact} is a polynomial system in $\Delta W$ and we do not solve it. Everything
below works with its linearisation, in which the constraint is replaced by $M(\Delta W) =
\Delta_\star$: the step map is the derivative of the left-hand side of \eqref{eq:exact} at
$\Delta W = 0$, so the two agree to first order. One further adjustment is needed. The gated
architectures do not in general admit every requested change --- $\Delta_\star$ need not lie in
$\mathrm{range}(M)$ --- so the best available request is its projection.

\begin{definition}[Reference step]
\label{def:ref}
The \emph{reference step} is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta W^{\mathrm{ref}}
  \;=\; \arg\min \; \|\Delta W\|_F
  \quad \text{subject to} \quad
  M(\Delta W) = \Pi\,\Delta_\star ,
  \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
  \Delta W^{\mathrm{ref}} = M^{+} \Delta_\star ,
  \label{eq:ref}
\end{equation}
where $M^{+}$ is the Moore--Penrose pseudo-inverse and $\|\cdot\|_F$ is the Euclidean norm on the
concatenated update.
\end{definition}

Minimum norm is not an arbitrary tie-break. The constraint in \eqref{eq:ref} fixes only the
first-order effect on the operator; any element of $\ker M$ may be added without changing it, and
such an addition moves the weights while leaving the function unchanged to first order. Requiring
the smallest update is what makes the reference a statement about the operator alone.

\begin{remark}[Relation to the conservation laws of gradient flow]
\label{rem:conserve}
$\ker M$ is the tangent space to the symmetry orbits of the parameterisation: for a linear chain,
$W_{\ell+1} \mapsto W_{\ell+1}G^{-1},\, W_\ell \mapsto G W_\ell$ leaves $P$ unchanged, and moving
along such an orbit is precisely an element of $\ker M$. A step lying in
$\mathrm{range}(M^\top) = (\ker M)^\perp$ therefore conserves the Noether charge associated with
that symmetry --- for a linear chain, the layer imbalance $W_{\ell+1}^\top W_{\ell+1} - W_\ell
W_\ell^\top$ --- which is the mechanism behind the conservation law of \citet{du2018algorithmic},
developed systematically by \citet{kunin2021neural} and characterised in full by
\citet{marcotte2023abide}. Both gradient descent, whose step is $M^\top \Delta_\star$, and the
reference, whose step is $M^{+}\Delta_\star = (M^\top M)^{+}M^\top \Delta_\star$, lie in
$\mathrm{range}(M^\top)$ by construction and so inherit it. We claim nothing new here; we record it
because it says what the reference step does \emph{not} do. It does not rebalance a network: an
unbalanced chain stays unbalanced under minimal displacement, and balancedness is a property of
minimising the weights themselves, which is a different problem. What is ours is the decomposition
as a \emph{measurement} --- which arms put mass in $\ker M$ and which do not --- reported in
Section~\ref{sec:e4}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The reference step is a preconditioned gradient step]
\label{rem:precond}
Since $M^{+} = (M^\top M)^{+} M^\top$ and $M^\top \Delta_\star = -\eta\,\nabla_W \mathcal{L}$ by
\eqref{eq:backprop}, Definition~\ref{def:ref} is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
  \Delta W^{\mathrm{ref}} \;=\; -\eta\,\big(M^\top M\big)^{+}\,\nabla_W \mathcal{L} .
  \label{eq:precond}
\end{equation}
The reference step is therefore a preconditioned gradient step, with the preconditioner given by
the pseudo-inverse of the Gram of the operator Jacobian. It belongs to the Gauss--Newton and
natural-gradient family, and we state this plainly rather than presenting it as a separate
construction. What distinguishes it is \emph{which} metric is pulled back. Gauss--Newton uses the
Jacobian of the \emph{outputs}; \eqref{eq:precond} uses the Jacobian of the \emph{operator}. These
coincide exactly for a deep linear network on whitened inputs --- there
$\sum_i x_i x_i^\top \propto I$ makes the two Gram matrices proportional, and the reference step
reduces to the exact natural gradient of \citet{bernacchia2018exact} --- and separate otherwise.
Measured on a depth-3 network, the cosine between the two steps is $1.000$ for deep linear with
whitened inputs, $0.881$ for deep linear with raw inputs, and $0.690$ and $0.388$ for ReLU with and
without whitening. The separation has a clear cause: prescribing $\Delta P(x)$ constrains the whole
Jacobian at each input, whereas prescribing $\Delta f(x)$ constrains only its action along $x$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{What we measure}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

\begin{definition}[Reachability]
\begin{equation}
  \alpha \;:=\; \frac{\|\Pi \Delta_\star\|}{\|\Delta_\star\|} \;\in\; [0,1].
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
$\alpha$ is the fraction of the requested operator change that the architecture admits at the
current weights. It depends on the model and the point, \emph{not} on the optimiser: no update
rule can realise more than $\alpha$. For a deep linear network with a single shared target,
$\alpha = 1$ generically; for gated networks $\alpha < 1$, and Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear-exp}
measures it.

\begin{definition}[Deflection]
Let $\Delta W_m$ be the update some optimiser would take from the current weights. We compare it
with the reference in each of the two spaces:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\cos_{\mathrm{op}} \;=\; \cos\!\big(\,\overbrace{M(\Delta W_m)}^{\text{operator change it causes}},\
      \overbrace{\Pi \Delta_\star}^{\text{best available}}\,\big)}_{\text{operator space}},
  \qquad
  \underbrace{\cos_{\mathrm{w}} \;=\; \cos\!\big(\,\overbrace{\Delta W_m}^{\text{step it takes}},\
      \overbrace{\Delta W^{\mathrm{ref}}}^{\text{step it should have taken}}\,\big)}_{\text{weight space}},
\end{equation}
together with the corresponding norm ratios $\|M(\Delta W_m)\|/\|\Pi\Delta_\star\|$ and
$\|\Delta W_m\|/\|\Delta W^{\mathrm{ref}}\|$.
\end{definition}

In words, there are only three objects and each is the answer to a plain question.
$\Delta_\star$ is \emph{what the loss asked the operator to do}. $\Pi\Delta_\star$ is \emph{how much
of that the architecture can actually deliver} --- and when the model is over-parameterised
relative to the batch, $M$ is surjective, $\Pi\Delta_\star=\Delta_\star$, $\alpha=1$, and this
distinction disappears entirely. $\Delta W^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is \emph{the weight update that delivers
it}, at least norm.

\textbf{Why both spaces.} The two cosines can disagree, and the disagreement is exactly what the
reference makes visible. Any element of $\ker M$ may be added to a weight update without changing
the operator at all, so a high $\cos_{\mathrm{op}}$ beside a low $\cos_{\mathrm{w}}$ says an
optimiser reached roughly the right operator motion \emph{by a different route through weight
space}. Asking that question requires a reference that is itself a weight update, which is what
distinguishes this construction from a purely functional comparison.

Both are cosines, hence invariant to the scale of $\Delta W_m$ and therefore \textbf{independent of
the learning rate}; only the norm ratios carry one, which is why they are reported separately. This
matters for the evidential status of the comparison: a claim expressed in these quantities cannot
be answered by asserting that a baseline was mis-tuned.

\begin{remark}[Fidelity is not quality]
\label{rem:fidelity}
$\Delta_\star = -\eta\,\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial P$ is steepest descent \emph{in operator
space}. On an anisotropic loss, steepest descent is itself an indirect route to the minimiser: it
removes the steep directions first and then advances slowly along the flat ones. Consequently an
optimiser may score \emph{lower} on $\cos_{\mathrm{op}}$ and still reach the optimum \emph{sooner}
--- a method whose step approximates a whitened or second-order direction will do exactly that.
$\cos_{\mathrm{op}}$ quantifies how far the factorisation deflects the step the loss asked for. It
is not a ranking of optimisers and must not be read as one.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[$\eta$ is an operator step, not a learning rate]
The target \eqref{eq:target} asks the operator to move by $\eta\|G\|$, so $\eta$ reads directly as
a fraction of $\|P(x)\|$ and is comparable across depths and architectures. A learning rate is not:
the same numerical value means different things at different depths because the map from weights
to operator changes with depth.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Gradient descent as a truncation]
\label{rem:krylov}
Solving \eqref{eq:ref} with a Krylov least-squares method started from zero, the first iterate is
proportional to $M^\top \Delta_\star$, which by \eqref{eq:backprop} is exactly the gradient descent
direction. The reference step is what the same solve returns on convergence. This is a convenient
implementation fact --- it means one routine produces both arms, and a truncation parameter
interpolates between them --- and we use it in Section~\ref{sec:s2}. We draw no further conclusion
from it.
\end{remark}

\section{What is already known}
\label{sec:sota}

\subsection{Dynamics and implicit bias in deep linear models}

The deep linear network is the standard setting in which implicit bias can be analysed rather than
only observed, because the function computed is a single matrix while the optimisation remains
non-convex. \citet{saxe2014exact} obtained exact solutions of the learning dynamics under gradient
flow from balanced and aligned initialisation with whitened inputs, exhibiting the sequential,
mode-by-mode learning that has since organised much of the field.
\citet{arora2018optimization} showed that over-parameterisation by depth acts as a preconditioner:
under gradient flow with a balanced initialisation, $W_{j+1}^\top W_{j+1} = W_j W_j^\top$, the
induced motion of the end-to-end matrix is
\begin{equation}
  \dot P \;=\; -\sum_{j=1}^{L}\big(PP^\top\big)^{\frac{L-j}{L}}\,G\,\big(P^\top P\big)^{\frac{j-1}{L}} ,
  \label{eq:arora}
\end{equation}
which on the singular values of $P$ reduces to a per-mode gain $L\,s^{2-2/L}$. Both hypotheses are
essential and neither is generic: \eqref{eq:arora} is a statement about gradient \emph{flow} from a
\emph{balanced} initialisation.

\citet{du2018algorithmic} showed that the layer-wise imbalance
$W_{\ell+1}^\top W_{\ell+1} - W_\ell W_\ell^\top$ is conserved under gradient flow for homogeneous
models, which is what makes balancedness a persistent property rather than an initial one, and
extends beyond the linear case. \citet{bah2022learning} identified the factor flow with a
Riemannian gradient flow on the manifold of fixed-rank matrices, and \citet{ablin2020deep} showed
that a deep \emph{orthogonally constrained} linear network is equivalent to a single layer, which
delimits how much of the depth effect survives when the factors are restricted.
\citet{wendin2026gradient} survey the gradient-flow equations for these models, and
\citet{cohen2024lectures} give a consolidated account of the area together with a list of
questions that remain open --- among them the behaviour of unbalanced initialisation beyond depth
two, which is the regime the reference step of Section~\ref{sec:stepmap} is designed to reach.

On the bias itself, \citet{gunasekar2017implicit} conjectured that gradient descent on a two-layer
factorisation with small initialisation and small steps converges to the minimum nuclear norm
solution, connecting the dynamics to the classical convex surrogate for rank
\citep{srebro2005maximum,recht2010guaranteed}. \citet{arora2019implicit} showed that depth
strengthens the tendency toward low rank in deep matrix factorisation, and
\citet{razin2020implicit} then showed that no norm --- nuclear or otherwise --- can explain the
bias in general, which redirected the field from characterising the endpoint by a penalty toward
characterising the trajectory. \citet{li2021towards} showed that the flow from infinitesimal
initialisation performs a greedy low-rank search, and \citet{jacot2021saddle} described the
saddle-to-saddle dynamics in which the effective rank increases by one at each saddle escape.
\citet{tarmoun2021understanding}, \citet{min2021explicit} and \citet{kunin2024get} analysed how the
initialisation --- in particular its imbalance --- controls both the convergence rate and the
resulting bias, showing that unbalanced initialisation is not a perturbation of the balanced theory
but a qualitatively different regime.

\subsection{Beyond the linear case}

For nonlinear networks the picture is less complete. \citet{timor2023implicit} established
regularisation toward rank minimisation in ReLU networks under stated conditions, and
\citet{jacot2023implicit} introduced a notion of rank for nonlinear functions under which large
depth induces a bias toward low rank. \citet{bantzis2025saddle} extended the saddle-escape analysis
to deep ReLU networks, finding a low-rank bias at the first escape, and \citet{rawal2026saddle} give
a theory of saddle escape in deep nonlinear networks. \citet{gatedlinear2022implicit} analysed
generalized gated linear networks, which is the closest published setting to the FGLN model of
Section~\ref{sec:models}. \citet{huh2021lowrank} documented the low-rank simplicity bias
empirically across architectures. A separate line studies the average gradient outer product as the
mechanism of feature learning \citep{radhakrishnan2024mechanism,beaglehole2024agop}.

Two further results bear directly on the objects we measure. \citet{pennington2017resurrecting}
showed that ReLU networks cannot achieve dynamical isometry --- the input--output Jacobian cannot
have a well-conditioned spectrum at depth --- which is why the reachability $\alpha$ of
Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics} cannot be expected to equal one for ReLU, and why a nonlinear
architecture that \emph{is} conditioned by construction (Section~\ref{sec:lookslinear}) is needed as
a control. \citet{wang2016extended} proposed the extended data Jacobian matrix as a diagnostic,
which is an operator-level measurement in our sense, though used for model comparison rather than
for scoring update rules. \citet{haas2026why} analyse how the Jacobian spectra of deep networks
separate with depth, and establish a conservation law for the gate-absorbed imbalance.

\subsection{Geometry-aware and second-order optimisation}

The reference step of Definition~\ref{def:ref} is a preconditioned gradient step
(Remark~\ref{rem:precond}), so it belongs squarely to this literature and we position it there
rather than apart from it. \citet{bernacchia2018exact} computed the exact natural gradient for deep
linear networks and observed that it annihilates precisely the depth-induced preconditioner in
\eqref{eq:arora}. That is the closest relation, and it is an identity rather than an analogy: on a
deep linear network with whitened inputs the reference step \emph{is} their natural gradient, since
the operator and output Gram matrices are then proportional. It separates from Gauss--Newton and
from natural gradient once the inputs are not whitened or the gates are input-dependent, because
prescribing $\Delta P(x)$ constrains the entire Jacobian at $x$ while prescribing $\Delta f(x)$
constrains only its action along $x$.

\citet{evans2026theory} give the closest published formulation of the underlying problem: they
derive minimal-norm weight perturbations achieving a specified change in \emph{output}, exactly for
a single layer, and compare against Lipschitz-based bounds for the multi-layer case; their purpose
is robustness certification rather than optimiser analysis. The difference from our setting is the
target: an output change constrains the network along one input, an operator change constrains its
action on every input at once. \citet{frerix2018proximal} recast
backpropagation as a sequence of proximal steps, and \citet{dufortlabbe2026layerwise} develop a
layerwise LQR view of geometry-aware optimisation. The distinction of purpose is worth stating
once: all of these are optimisation methods proposed to train faster, whereas we use the algebra as
a \emph{measuring instrument}, and Remark~\ref{rem:fidelity} explains why the two goals must not be
conflated.

\subsection{The gap}

Across this literature the bias is inferred from endpoints, and its architectural and algorithmic
components are not separated. Statements of the form ``depth strengthens the low-rank bias'' are
established by varying depth and observing the endpoint; they do not distinguish a change in what
the architecture \emph{permits} from a change in what the optimiser \emph{does} within what is
permitted. The reference step makes that separation computable, and the two diagnostics of
Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics} report the two halves.

\section{Part I --- Deep linear networks}
\label{sec:linear}

In a deep linear network the gates are the identity, so $P = W_L \cdots W_1$ is a single matrix
shared by every input. The target reduces to one matrix, $\Delta_\star = -\eta\,
\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial P$, and the step map \eqref{eq:M} carries weight updates into the
space of $n_L \times n_0$ matrices.

This is the regime in which the framework can be \emph{seen} rather than only measured. With a
two-dimensional operator the trajectory of $P$ under any optimiser can be drawn exactly, against
a loss whose contours are exact ellipses, and every difference between the paths is attributable
to how each rule converts a weight step into an operator step --- since all of them descend the
same quadratic in $P$. That is the subject of this part. The substantive comparisons between
optimisers are deferred to Part II, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:whatchanges}: a deep
linear network with enough samples pins its single shared $P$, so all methods that fit the data
must agree, and there is nothing at the endpoint to distinguish.

\subsection{Experiments}
\label{sec:linear-exp}

\subsubsection{E2 --- Operator-space trajectories}
\label{sec:e2}

\paragraph{Motivation and question.}
The diagnostics of Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics} are per-step scalars. \emph{What do the
trajectories themselves look like, and is the separation between rules visible as geometry rather
than only as a summary statistic?} A deep linear chain answers it exactly: the operator is a single
$1\times2$ matrix, so the path can be drawn without projection, and the loss is exactly quadratic,
so the geometry is not in question.

\paragraph{Why operator space and not weight space.}
This is not a presentational choice. A model whose \emph{weight} space is two-dimensional has a
rank-one step map $M = [\,w_2\ \ w_1\,]$, whence $M^{+} \propto M^\top$ and gradient descent
coincides with the reference \emph{exactly} --- measured at $\cos = 1.0000000000$. No
parameterisation effect can appear in such a picture. With $L$ layers feeding a two-dimensional
operator, $M$ has rank two and the rules separate. Every difference visible below is therefore
attributable to how each rule converts a weight step into an operator step, since all of them
descend the same quadratic in $P$.

\paragraph{Setup.}
A bias-free linear chain of depth $L$ and width $32$ mapping two features to one output, so
$P \in \mathbb{R}^{1\times2}$: $X \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times 2}$ with feature scales $(1.0, 0.35)$
so the loss is anisotropic, $y = Xw$ with $w$ drawn once, float64, $300$ steps. Depths
$L \in \{2, 32\}$ and initialisations $\{$Xavier, orthogonal$\}$. Eight optimisers: gradient
descent, Adam, the reference, Muon, Shampoo, SOAP, K-FAC and heavy ball.

Two configuration notes. He initialisation is omitted: applied to a \emph{linear} chain it assumes
a rectifier halves the variance and there is nothing to halve it, so the gain is $2$ per layer and
the operator reaches $\|P\|_\infty = 1.1\times10^{4}$ at $L=32$, from which no optimiser in the grid
recovers. Looks-linear initialisation is also omitted: the $[\,O \mid -O\,]$ construction requires
CReLU's doubled feature dimension (Section~\ref{sec:lookslinear}), so on a linear chain it reduces
to the orthogonal case and contributes no separate panel.

The reference here is \eqref{eq:ref} solved to convergence --- a damped minimum-norm solve of the
same minimum-displacement problem --- rather than the Krylov truncation at $k = 50$ used as the
practical solver in Part II. On a deep linear chain the step map is largely null, measured at $83\%$
nullity at $L=6$, width $8$, and a truncated solve there reflects the solver's iteration budget
rather than the rule.

Each arm's step size is swept over a grid spanning $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-1}$ and the run drawn is the
one that descends most directly among those reaching within a factor of two of that arm's best
final loss. Selecting purely by lowest loss admits step sizes that overshoot and oscillate across
the optimum: such a run lands low, but its path is a zigzag that describes the tuning rather than
the rule.

\paragraph{Measurement.}
For the figure, the operator path itself. Separately, on a depth series at width $8$ with five
seeds, the realised operator change $\Delta P$ and its alignment with the request,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{align} \;:=\; \cos\!\Big(\Delta P,\ -\tfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial P}\Big),
  \label{eq:align}
\end{equation}
from which we report the fraction of steps on which alignment is negative --- on which the step
moved the operator \emph{against} the direction the loss asked for.

\paragraph{Conclusions.}
\emph{The rules separate geometrically, and the separation is not a difference in destination.} All
eight reach the least squares solution in all four panels; what differs is the route. The reference,
gradient descent and K-FAC descend along nearly the same arc --- which is the visual form of K-FAC's
predicted closeness to the projection (Section~\ref{sec:sota}) --- while SOAP and heavy ball take
wide excursions transverse to it before turning in, and Muon takes a distinct intermediate path.
Since every arm minimises the same quadratic in $P$, those excursions are produced entirely by how
each rule maps a weight step into an operator step.

\emph{Depth widens the spread.} At $L=2$ the arms are bunched; at $L=32$ they fan out, and the
step sizes that survive selection fall by two to three orders of magnitude for the elementwise
methods (Adam from $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-5}$ at Xavier) while the reference's target size is unchanged
at $\eta = 0.3$ across both depths and both initialisations. The reference has no step size to
retune because its scale is set by the requested operator change rather than by the curvature of the
parameterisation.

\emph{Alignment is fidelity, not optimisation quality.} Adam moves the operator against the
requested direction on a substantial fraction of steps at depth, in every seed, while gradient
descent and the reference essentially never do. Yet at $L=32$ Adam reaches
$\|P - P^\star\| = 0.0000$ while the reference reaches $0.1437$: the worse-aligned method is the
faster one. Remark~\ref{rem:fidelity} states this distinction and it is enforced throughout the
paper.

\section{Part II --- Gated networks}
\label{sec:nonlinear}

\subsection{What changes}
\label{sec:whatchanges}

Three things change at once when the gates become input-dependent, and it is worth separating
them.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{The operator is no longer shared.} Each input has its own $P(x)$, and the target
        \eqref{eq:target} asks for a different operator change at each one. A single weight update
        must serve all of these requests simultaneously.
  \item \textbf{The requests are not jointly realisable.} Since one $\Delta W$ produces the whole
        collection $M(\Delta W)$, and the collection has far more degrees of freedom than
        $\Delta W$, in general $\Delta_\star \notin \mathrm{range}(M)$ and $\alpha < 1$. The
        reference step realises the projection $\Pi \Delta_\star$, which is the most any optimiser
        can do.
  \item \textbf{Each sample constrains only a rank-one slice of its own operator.} Fitting the
        data requires $P(x_i) x_i = y_i$, which fixes the action of $P(x_i)$ along $x_i$ and leaves
        the remaining directions free. This is why gated networks can exhibit genuine differences
        between optimisers at the same training loss, while a deep linear network with enough
        samples cannot: there the single shared $P$ is pinned by the data, and all methods that fit
        must agree.
\end{enumerate}

Point 3 is the reason the endpoint comparisons of Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear-exp} are posed for
gated networks and not for deep linear ones.

\subsection{CReLU and looks-linear initialisation: a conditioned nonlinear control}
\label{sec:lookslinear}

The ReLU network confounds two effects that we wish to separate: it is nonlinear, and at depth it
is ill-conditioned, necessarily so \citep{pennington2017resurrecting}. To vary gating and
conditioning independently we need a network that is genuinely nonlinear yet perfectly conditioned
by construction. The concatenated rectifier of \eqref{eq:crelu}, introduced by
\citet{shang2016crelu} to exploit the opposite-phase filter pairs observed in trained
convolutional networks, provides one.

Write the layer weight in block form $W = [\,O \mid -O\,]$ with $O$ orthogonal --- the
\emph{looks-linear} initialisation of \citet{balduzzi2017shattered}, introduced there to keep
gradients from shattering with depth by making the network exactly linear at initialisation. Then for every $z$,
\begin{equation}
  W D(z) \;=\; O\,\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{1}[z>0]) \;+\; O\,\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{1}[z<0]) \;=\; O ,
\end{equation}
because the two indicator masks are complementary. Hence $P(x) = O_L \cdots O_1$ is orthogonal, at
every input and at \emph{any} depth: all singular values equal one, and the operator is identical
across inputs at initialisation. The network is nonetheless a genuine CReLU network --- the gates
are real, they differ between inputs as soon as training moves the weights away from the
looks-linear point, and no reparameterisation makes it linear.

This gives the control the rest of Part II requires: CReLU with looks-linear initialisation is
nonlinear and perfectly conditioned; ReLU with a standard random initialisation is nonlinear and
ill-conditioned at depth; the deep linear network of Part I is linear. Differences attributed to
gating can then be distinguished from differences attributable to conditioning.

\subsection{Solver S2: least squares on the step map}
\label{sec:s2}

For gated networks we solve \eqref{eq:ref} directly as a least-squares problem,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta W^{\mathrm{ref}} \;=\; \arg\min_{\Delta W} \; \big\|M(\Delta W) - \Delta_\star\big\|^2 ,
  \qquad \text{minimum-norm solution},
\end{equation}
using LSQR \citep{paige1982lsqr}, which requires only products with $M$ and $M^\top$ and never
forms the matrix. By \eqref{eq:M} and its adjoint, both products are batched matrix
multiplications over layers and inputs, so the solve costs $O(k)$ applications of the forward and
backward context products for $k$ iterations. By Remark~\ref{rem:krylov}, $k = 1$ returns the
gradient descent direction and large $k$ returns the reference step, so a single routine produces
both arms.

\paragraph{A numerical caveat that must not be skipped.}
On these step maps, LSQR \emph{diverges after it has converged}: the Lanczos basis loses
orthogonality, and the iterate grows without bound while the least-squares residual remains at its
converged value. Running to a fixed large iteration count therefore returns a meaningless result
with no error raised. Two natural stopping tests do not detect this. The quantity $\bar\phi$
maintained internally by the LSQR recursion is monotonically non-increasing by construction and so
never signals anything. The residual $\|M(\Delta W) - \Delta_\star\|$ is blind to the component of
the failure that lies in $\ker M$, which is precisely where the iterate grows. The normal-equation
residual $\|M^\top(M(\Delta W) - \Delta_\star)\|$ detects both, and is the stopping rule we use; it
is not monotone, so the best iterate is retained rather than the last, and its attained value is
recorded alongside every measurement. We report quantitative diagnostics in
Appendix~\ref{app:numerics}.

\subsubsection{Common configuration}

Unless an experiment states otherwise, all of Part II uses the following.

\paragraph{Task.} MNIST-1D \citep{greydanus2020mnist1d}: $40$-dimensional inputs, $10$ classes,

The task is small enough that every arm can be run to interpolation on a full hyper-parameter grid
with several seeds, which is what makes the comparisons below matched rather than budget-limited.

\paragraph{Model.} Bias-free chain of depth $L = 8$ and width $128$, He initialisation. The main
grid is ReLU and CReLU, the two gated architectures that carry the comparison; leaky ReLU
($\alpha = 0.1$) is added wherever unit death would otherwise confound the reading, and each
experiment states whether it is included. Deep linear is not part of this grid, for the reason in
Section~\ref{sec:whatchanges}: its single shared operator is pinned by the data, so all methods
that fit must agree. Bias-freedom is what makes
$f(x) = P(x)x$ exact, so the operator is the object the model actually computes rather than an
approximation to it.

\paragraph{Arms.} \emph{gradient descent} and \emph{Adam}, each over a learning-rate grid, and the
\emph{reference} step of Section~\ref{sec:s2} over a grid of target sizes $\eta$ at truncation
$k = 50$. Training runs for $12000$ steps, which is past interpolation for every arm.

\paragraph{Selection and scoring.} A run is summarised by its \emph{stable test accuracy}: the mean
test accuracy over the final quarter of its probes. Hyper-parameters are chosen by this same
statistic. This matters: these trajectories oscillate after interpolation, and selecting a run at
the peak of its validation curve while scoring it by a stable statistic --- or the reverse ---
introduces a bias of the same order as the differences being measured. Figure~\ref{fig:selection}
shows the size of the effect: the gap between peak-selected and last-quarter-mean test accuracy,
plotted against the swing of each run over the second half of training. Every number in Part II is
selected and scored by the same estimator.

$\ker M$, which is exactly the direction an undamped iteration drifts in.

Reported uncertainties are standard deviations across seeds.

\subsubsection{E3 --- How much of the request is reachable at all}
\label{sec:e3}

\paragraph{Motivation and question.}
Reachability is a ceiling: a property of the architecture and the current weights that no optimiser
can exceed. \emph{Is the gap between optimisers a gap in what they deliver, or in what the
architecture permits?} The question must be settled before any optimiser is scored, because a low
score against a low ceiling means something entirely different from a low score against a high one.

\paragraph{Setup.} The common configuration, probed along the trajectory of each arm.

\paragraph{Measurement.}
$\alpha := \|\Pi \Delta_\star\| / \|\Delta_\star\| \in [0,1]$, the fraction of the requested
operator change that lies in $\mathrm{range}(M)$. Being a projection ratio, $\alpha > 1$ is
impossible and serves as an independent check on the solve.

\paragraph{Status.} \textsc{done}.

\paragraph{Results.}
$\alpha$ is essentially $1$ everywhere: $1.0000$ for gradient descent in all three gated
architectures, $0.9942$--$0.9999$ for the reference, and $0.8005$--$0.9997$ for Adam, the last of
these along Adam's own trajectory where the weights have travelled furthest from initialisation.

\paragraph{Conclusions.}
At this width the model is over-parameterised relative to the batch, the requested operator change
is almost entirely reachable, and the ceiling is not what separates the arms. Every difference
reported below is a difference in what an optimiser \emph{does} with an available direction, not in
what the architecture allows. This is a negative result about a confound, and it is what licenses
the rest of Part II.

\[

  \text{reference } (0.55\text{--}0.58) \;<\;

and it is the same in all three gated architectures.

does help, relative to the rule that differs from it only by Krylov truncation.

It does not close the gap to Adam, and this is the central negative result of the paper. The most
operator-faithful rule is not the best-generalising one; the \emph{least} faithful rule is. Sections
\ref{sec:e13}--\ref{sec:e16} pursue candidate explanations for that inversion --- when the advantage
is created, whether more training closes it, whether momentum is the hidden mechanism, and whether
robustness separates the arms --- and eliminate each.

\subsubsection{E6 --- Calibrating the scale: what do these accuracies mean?}
\label{sec:e6}

\paragraph{Motivation and question.}
A $2$--$6$ point improvement is only interesting against a scale. \emph{Where do these accuracies
sit relative to methods that use no learned representation at all?}

\paragraph{Setup.} $k$-nearest neighbours on the same split, Euclidean distance on raw inputs,
$k \in \{1,3,5,10,20,50\}$; multinomial logistic regression on raw inputs; and for context the
published MNIST-1D baselines for an MLP and a CNN.

\paragraph{Status.} \textsc{done}.

\paragraph{Results.}
$k$-NN reaches $0.552$ at $k=1$, $0.563$ at $k=5$, $0.580$ at $k=10$ and $0.458$ at $k=50$.
Logistic regression on raw inputs reaches $0.32$. For reference, a tuned MLP reaches $\approx 0.68$
and a CNN $\approx 0.94$ on this task. Placing our arms on the same line:
\[
 \text{logistic } 0.32 \;<\;
 \text{gd } 0.51\text{--}0.53 \;<\;
 \text{reference } 0.55\text{--}0.58 \;\approx\; \text{$k$-NN } 0.58 \;<\;
 \text{Adam } 0.61\text{--}0.66 \;<\; \text{MLP } 0.68 \;<\; \text{CNN } 0.94 .
\]

\paragraph{Conclusions.}
The reference generalises at roughly $k$-nearest-neighbour level. It clears logistic regression by
a wide margin, so it is learning a genuinely nonlinear function, but it does not exceed what a
memorising predictor achieves on this task. Adam does. This calibration is worth stating plainly
because it bounds how much the reference's improvement over gradient descent can be claimed to
mean: the improvement is real and consistent, and it lands at the level of a method with no
representation learning at all.

\subsubsection{E7 --- The bias dial: interpolating from gradient descent to the reference}

\subsubsection{E9 --- Operator rank and operator diversity}
\label{sec:e9}

\paragraph{Motivation and question.}
$P(x)$ is a linear map per input. Two different quantities can be called its complexity: the rank of
a single $P(x)$, and how much $P(x)$ \emph{varies} across inputs. \emph{Do the arms differ on
either, and are the two the same story?}

\paragraph{Setup.} The common configuration, final checkpoint, evaluated on $256$ training inputs
for the pointwise measure and $512$ for the diversity measure.

\paragraph{Measurement.}
Pointwise operator rank is the participation ratio
$\mathrm{PR}(P) = (\sum_i \sigma_i)^2 / \sum_i \sigma_i^2$ of the singular values of $P(x)$,
averaged over inputs with annihilated inputs excluded and reported separately. Operator diversity is
the participation ratio of the stacked, centred $\{\mathrm{vec}\,P(x_i)\}$, which measures how many
directions the operator explores across the dataset.

\paragraph{Status.} \textsc{done}.

\paragraph{Results.}
Pointwise rank at the final checkpoint: gradient descent $5.78$--$6.54$, Adam $1.98$--$3.26$,
reference $1.56$--$2.70$. Operator diversity, against a ceiling of $512$ and the value at
initialisation:

      & Adam             & $3.11$ & $104.5$ & $39.5\%$ \\
      & reference        & $7.29$ & $53.2$  & $20.1\%$ \\
\midrule
ReLU  & initialisation   & ---    & $241.2$ & $100\%$ \\
      & gradient descent & $5.70$ & $207.4$ & $86.0\%$ \\

\paragraph{Conclusions.}
The two measures tell different stories and must not be conflated. Gradient descent keeps a

across arms are confounded with the operator norm the arms arrive at.

\subsubsection{E11 --- Where the linear regions sit}

This is the clearest positive result in Part II and it sharpens the open question: what does Adam do
in the first few thousand steps that the reference does not, and why is it irreversible?

The headline answer to the question is negative: more time does not close the reference's gap, and
the ordering does not reverse.

\subsubsection{E15 --- Isolating the momentum effect}
\label{sec:e15}

\paragraph{Motivation and question.}
The deflection diagnostic of E4 compares a \emph{single} step against the request made at that same
point. That is the right comparison for a memoryless rule, but it is systematically unkind to a rule
with memory. Adam carries a first-moment average with $\beta_1 = 0.9$, so its current step is a
weighted average of roughly the last ten gradients; it therefore \emph{must} deviate from the
current request, not because its direction is wrong but because it is integrating. Its near-zero
$\cos_{op}$ is thus not, on its own, evidence about the quality of the direction it takes.

This raises a concrete possibility that the diagnostic cannot see: \emph{Adam may be performing
something equivalent to momentum at the operator level --- accumulating a coherent operator motion
across steps --- while every individual step scores as unfaithful.} If so, the framework would be
missing the mechanism entirely rather than measuring it.

The question this experiment answers is therefore: \emph{is momentum the mechanism?} Adding momentum
directly to the operator step isolates that one ingredient, holding target, solver, step size and
seeds fixed. If accumulated operator motion is what Adam's advantage rests on, the operator rule
with momentum should capture it.

\paragraph{Setup.}
The reference step is smoothed directly,
\begin{equation}
  u_t = \beta\, u_{t-1} + (1-\beta)\, \Delta W^{\mathrm{ref}}_t, \qquad W_{t+1} = W_t + u_t ,
  \label{eq:opmom}
\end{equation}
with \emph{no} learning rate: the operator rule has none, since the step size enters through $\eta$
inside $\Delta_\star$. The factor $(1-\beta)$ keeps $\|u_t\|$ on the scale of a single reference
step, so $\beta$ changes the direction the step is smoothed towards and not its magnitude, and
$\beta = 0$ recovers the unmodified reference exactly. This is momentum on the \emph{realised
operator displacement} rather than on the gradient, which is what makes it the operator-level
analogue of Adam's first moment. ReLU and CReLU, $\eta \in \{0.5, 1\}$, $k = 50$,
$\beta \in \{0, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99\}$, three seeds: $48$ cells.

\paragraph{Measurement.}
Stable test accuracy, paired against the $\beta = 0$ control on the same seed, together with
$\cos_{op}$ and $\cos_w$ over the same cells and the final training loss.

\paragraph{Status.} \textsc{done}.

\paragraph{Results.}

\paragraph{Conclusions.}
\emph{Momentum is not the mechanism.} No $\beta$ beats the $\beta = 0$ control by more than noise,
and $\beta = 0.99$ costs $0.05$--$0.07$ where the effect is largest. Since every cell interpolates,
this is a generalisation effect and not a failure to fit. Accumulating operator motion across steps,
which is the operator-level analogue of what Adam's first moment does, does not reproduce Adam's
advantage. Whatever Adam is doing, it is not that.

\emph{The measurement caveat is confirmed and quantified.} Momentum does exactly what the framing
predicted it would do to the diagnostic: it drives single-step $\cos_{op}$ down by an order of
magnitude, from $0.80$--$0.88$ to $0.09$--$0.30$, purely by carrying stale displacement. The reason
is structural --- $\Delta W^{\mathrm{ref}}_t$ lies in $\mathrm{range}(M_t^\top)$, but $M_t$ moves
with the weights, so a stale $u_{t-1}$ has a component in $\ker M_t$ at the current point. So a low
single-step deflection is genuinely ambiguous between "this rule takes a poor direction" and "this
rule has memory", and Adam's near-zero value cannot be read as the former without this control.

The two findings together close the loop. Momentum \emph{can} produce a low $\cos_{op}$ without any
benefit, so the diagnostic's unkindness to rules with memory is real; and momentum applied to the
operator rule produces no benefit either, so the hypothesis that Adam's advantage is hidden
accumulated operator motion is eliminated rather than merely unmeasured. A useful consequence for
reading the rest of Part II: any conclusion drawn from $\cos_{op}$ about a momentum-carrying rule
should be understood as a statement about its single steps, not about its trajectory.

\subsubsection{E16 --- Adversarial robustness}
\label{sec:e16}

\paragraph{Motivation and question.}
Clean accuracy is one axis. \emph{Do the arms differ in the robustness of the function they arrive
at, and if so does that separate them where the structural measures did not?} A method that places
its partition on the data (E11) and under-uses its gating (E8) might plausibly produce a flatter,
more robust function.

\paragraph{Setup.}
Every $(\text{architecture}, \text{arm}, \text{hyper-parameter})$ cell at the common configuration,
up to three seeds, scored at the final checkpoint with no selection of any kind.

\paragraph{Measurement.}
Accuracy under an $L_\infty$-bounded attack of radius $\epsilon$, $50$ steps of size
$2.5\epsilon/50$ from a uniform random start, two restarts. The attack ascends the margin
$\max_{j \ne y} z_j - z_y$ rather than the cross-entropy. This choice is essential rather than
incidental: the networks here are bias-free and positively homogeneous, so nothing bounds the logit
scale along an interpolating trajectory, and the measured logit margins differ between arms by two
orders of magnitude ($0.78$ to $14.1$ in the table below). A cross-entropy attack saturates against
large-margin networks and reports them as robust; the margin objective is scale-covariant, so
multiplying all logits by $c > 0$ multiplies it by $c$ and leaves its gradient direction unchanged.
We report robustness as a \emph{ratio} to clean accuracy, since the arms differ by up to $13$ points
of clean accuracy.

\subsubsection{E18 --- Preconditioned and orthogonalised comparators}
\label{sec:e18}

\paragraph{Motivation and question.}
The reference step is a preconditioner, $-\eta (M^\top M)^{+}\nabla_W \mathcal{L}$, and the natural
objection is that it may be a known one in disguise. \emph{How does it relate empirically to the
geometry-aware optimisers of Section~\ref{sec:sota}?} K-FAC is the sharpest case: in the
isotropic deep-linear setting it diagonalises in the same bases as the reference and differs only in
its denominator, $(\sigma_A^2 + \lambda)(\sigma_B^2 + \lambda)$ against
$\sigma_A^2 \sigma_B^2 + \lambda$, so it is predicted to be the closest baseline and to depart from
the projection precisely in the high-damping regime used for stability at depth.

\paragraph{Setup.}
Muon, Shampoo, SOAP, K-FAC and heavy ball, added as arms to the common configuration at $L = 8$,
ReLU and CReLU, three seeds, over per-optimiser learning-rate grids. Each is implemented to match
its published form; K-FAC reads its Kronecker factors off the layer chain rather than estimating
them in parameter space, which is what makes the prediction above testable.

\paragraph{Measurement.}
The full diagnostic set of E4 and E5: $\cos_{op}$, $\cos_w$, the $\ker M$ fraction, operator rank
and stable test accuracy, so that each comparator takes its place in the same table as the three
main arms.

\paragraph{Status.} \textsc{queued}. This sweep runs after E17 completes; no numbers yet.

\paragraph{What the result would mean.}
If K-FAC's $\cos_{op}$ sits close to the reference's while the elementwise methods sit near Adam's,
the framework's grouping --- two-sided methods can act on the operator's geometry, one-sided ones
cannot --- is supported by an independent test. If Muon, which orthogonalises its update and is
therefore perfectly conditioned in \emph{weight} space, nonetheless shows low $\cos_{op}$, that
separates weight-space conditioning from operator-space faithfulness, which is the distinction the
paper rests on.

\subsubsection{E20 --- Noise in the step, and where it is allowed to live}
\label{sec:e20}

\paragraph{Motivation and question.}
Two observations from the experiments above point the same way. First, the reference generalises
\emph{better at larger target size}: on ReLU its accuracy rises monotonically with $\eta$, from
$0.524$ at $\eta = 0.1$ to $0.570$ at $\eta = 1$, and the same ordering holds on CReLU and leaky
(E7). Second, larger $\eta$ crosses more nonlinearities: gate churn over the same cells rises from
$0.0070$ to $0.0303$. A step large enough to move pre-activations across zero changes which linear
region the model is in, which injects a form of stochasticity into the trajectory that a small step
does not have.

That suggests a mechanism rather than an accident, and it is one with a long literature behind it.
Noise in the update is a classical route to flat minima --- regions of weight space over which the
error is approximately constant \citep{hochreiter1997flat} --- whether it arises from Langevin
dynamics \citep{welling2011sgld}, from explicit gradient-noise injection
\citep{neelakantan2015adding}, from the minibatch noise of SGD itself
\citep{smith2020generalization}, or from deliberately anticorrelated perturbations
\citep{orvieto2022anticorrelated}.

\emph{So: does injecting noise into the reference step improve its generalisation, and if it does,
is the benefit coming from perturbing the function or from relocating the weights?} The second half
is the part this framework can answer and isotropic noise injection cannot.

\paragraph{The decomposition, and why it is the interesting measurement.}
Weight space splits as $\mathrm{range}(M^\top) \oplus \ker M$. A perturbation in $\ker M$ moves the
weights while leaving $M(\Delta W) = 0$: to first order the operator, and therefore the function the
network computes, is \emph{unchanged}. That is exactly Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's flat region,
identified constructively rather than searched for. A perturbation in $\mathrm{range}(M^\top)$ is
the complement and changes the operator.

This gives three arms that inject the same amount of noise in different places:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta W \;=\; \Delta W^{\mathrm{ref}} \;+\;
  \varepsilon\,\frac{\|\Delta W^{\mathrm{ref}}\|}{\|\Pi_\bullet g\|}\,\Pi_\bullet g ,
  \qquad g \sim \mathcal N(0, I),
  \label{eq:opnoise}
\end{equation}
with $\Pi_\bullet$ the identity (\textsc{iso}), the projection onto $\ker M$ (\textsc{ker}), or the
projection onto $\mathrm{range}(M^\top)$ (\textsc{range}). Scaling by
$\|\Delta W^{\mathrm{ref}}\|$ makes $\varepsilon$ dimensionless and comparable across $\eta$.

The three are separated as intended. Measured at $\varepsilon = 0.1$ on ReLU, the operator
perturbation induced relative to the reference step's own operator motion is $0.137$ for
\textsc{iso}, $0.017$ for \textsc{ker} and $0.324$ for \textsc{range}: the \textsc{ker} arm moves
the weights by the same amount while moving the function by an order of magnitude less.

\paragraph{How large is $\ker M$, and what that implies for the design.}
The three arms are only three different experiments if the projections actually move the noise
somewhere it would not otherwise be. Measuring the fraction of a random direction that already
lies in $\ker M$:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
fraction of a random $g$ in $\ker M$ & $L=2$ & $L=8$ & $L=32$ \\
\midrule
ReLU  & $0.02$ & $0.63$ & $0.94$ \\
CReLU & $0.13$ & $0.81$ & $0.96$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The null space grows quickly with depth, and this reshapes the experiment. At $L=8$ isotropic
noise is \emph{already} $63$--$81\%$ function-preserving, so the \textsc{ker} arm is a refinement
of \textsc{iso} rather than a contrast with it, and the two may be hard to tell apart. The arm
that genuinely differs is \textsc{range}, which concentrates all of the perturbation into the
minority of directions that change the operator. At $L=32$ the point is sharper still: isotropic
noise there is $94$--$96\%$ confined to $\ker M$, so the cheap arm is essentially the
function-preserving experiment already, and only the \textsc{range} projection has to be paid for.

This is not a detail of implementation. It says that in a deep network, most of what any noise
injection method does is move weights without changing the function --- and that the flat-minima
reading of noise injection is, in these models, a statement about the majority of the perturbation
rather than an incidental part of it.

\paragraph{Setup.}
MNIST-1D, the common configuration, ReLU, $\eta = 1$, $k = 50$,
$\varepsilon \in \{0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3\}$, two seeds, with $\varepsilon = 0$ rerun inside the same
sweep as the control. The \textsc{ker} and \textsc{range} arms require a projection at every step
and are correspondingly more expensive, so they follow the isotropic pilot.

\paragraph{Measurement.}
Stable test accuracy against $\varepsilon$, paired to the $\varepsilon = 0$ control; final training
loss and accuracy, to establish whether any change is a generalisation effect or a fitting one; and
gate churn, to test whether injected noise reproduces the gate-crossing behaviour that larger $\eta$
produces.

\paragraph{Status.} \textsc{partial}. The isotropic pilot is running; the \textsc{ker} and
\textsc{range} arms are queued. No numbers yet.

\paragraph{What the possible outcomes would mean.}
This is stated in advance because the experiment is cheap to over-read after the fact.

If \textsc{iso} helps, we reproduce a known effect \citep{smith2020generalization} with a different
base optimiser, which is a useful sanity check on the reference rule but not a contribution.

If \textsc{ker} helps as much as \textsc{iso}, the benefit is available \emph{without changing the
function at all}: the gain would come from where in the level set the weights sit, which is the
flat-minima hypothesis in its purest measurable form and is not a statement isotropic injection can
make.

If \textsc{range} helps and \textsc{ker} does not, the benefit requires perturbing the function, and
the flat-minima reading of the $\eta$ trend is wrong --- the effect would be closer to label-noise
or implicit-regularisation-through-perturbation than to basin geometry.

If nothing helps, the $\eta$ trend of E7 is a step-size effect rather than a noise effect, which is
the confound this experiment exists to remove: larger $\eta$ changes both the step size and the
amount of gate crossing, and only a fixed-$\eta$ noise sweep separates them.

Two groups emerge. Switching and region concentration separate the \emph{reference from both
baselines}; operator rank and diversity separate \emph{gradient descent from both}. Neither grouping
matches the accuracy ordering, and in particular nothing here explains the gradient-descent--Adam
gap, which is the largest of the three: on region concentration the two are statistically
indistinguishable ($t = +0.1$ on CReLU), and on switching they differ in the wrong direction.

$\cos_{op}$ is the only measure that cleanly separates all three, and it orders them \emph{inversely}
to accuracy. E15 shows that this inversion must not be read as a causal claim, and why: the
diagnostic compares single steps, so a rule with memory scores low for a reason unrelated to the
quality of its direction. Adam carries a first moment; operator momentum reproduces its low
$\cos_{op}$ without reproducing any of its benefit, and adding accumulated operator motion to the
reference rule does not recover Adam's advantage. So the framework's central diagnostic is a genuine
and reproducible ordering of optimisers that does \emph{not} reduce to generalisation, and one whose
value for momentum-carrying rules is a statement about their steps rather than their trajectories.

Two cautions on reading the table. Operator rank and robustness vary more \emph{within} an arm
across its hyper-parameter grid than \emph{between} arms at their best settings, so a per-arm row
understates the variation. And the reference's $\cos_{op}$ row is partly a statement about Krylov
truncation at $k = 50$ rather than about the rule, as E4 and E17 discuss.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Implicit bias is usually studied through the solutions an optimiser reaches. We have argued that it
can be studied through the steps it takes, provided one is willing to compute, at each point, the
weight update that would have realised what the loss asked of the operator. That reference step is
available in closed form for deep linear chains, where it is exact even at parameter values that
stop every derivative-based method, and as a least-squares solve for gated networks, where the
request is no longer fully realisable and the shortfall is itself a measurable property of the
architecture.

The resulting decomposition separates two things that endpoint studies necessarily conflate: what
the architecture permits, and what the optimiser does within what is permitted. The first is
optimiser-independent; the second is scale-invariant, and therefore free of the tuning objections
that comparisons between optimisers usually attract.

\emph{[Summary of findings to be inserted once the measurements of
Sections~\ref{sec:linear-exp} and \ref{sec:nonlinear-exp} are complete.]}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The reference step is defined by a first-order model of how weights move the operator, and is
therefore meaningful only for steps small enough to stay in that regime; we report the step sizes
at which this holds. The least-squares solve becomes ill-conditioned with depth, and the
weight-space diagnostic $\cos_{\mathrm{w}}$ in particular is reliable only where the solve attains
the minimum-norm solution, which we verify per measurement rather than assume. Finally,
Remark~\ref{rem:fidelity} bears repeating: the diagnostics measure fidelity to the operator
gradient, which is not the same as optimisation quality, and nothing here should be read as a claim
that the reference step is a better training algorithm.

\section{Hardware, cost, and what each method costs}
\label{app:cost}

\subsection{Hardware and software}

All experiments ran on a single workstation: one NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada Generation
($47.4$\,GiB, $142$ SMs, compute capability $8.9$), an Intel Core i9-14900 ($32$ threads) and
$123$\,GiB of host memory, under Linux 6.17, CUDA 13.0, PyTorch 2.11 and Python 3.13. No
distributed or multi-GPU training was used anywhere, and no experiment in this paper requires it.

One property of this device shapes several design decisions below. Measured on $2048^2$ matrix
products, it sustains $36.1$ TFLOP/s in float32 and $0.99$ TFLOP/s in float64, a ratio of
$37{:}1$. Every diagnostic that has to be trusted to many digits --- the reachability check
$\alpha \le 1$, the normal-equation residual, the structural identities --- runs in float64 and
therefore runs at roughly one thirty-seventh of peak. This is affordable at MNIST-1D scale and is
not at CIFAR-10 scale, which is why the probe there runs in float32 with its solve quality
recorded rather than assumed (Section~\ref{sec:e19}).

\paragraph{How this scales, which is the part that matters.} The reference's relative cost
\emph{falls sharply with depth}: $661\times$ gradient descent at $L=2$, $152\times$ at $L=8$,
$55\times$ at $L=32$. The reason is that the step solves $M^{+}\Delta_\star$ by $k$ Krylov
iterations, each of which is a handful of small kernels rather than one large one, so at these
sizes it is launch-bound rather than arithmetic-bound. Going from $L=2$ to $L=32$ multiplies the
parameter count by $78$ ($6.4$k to $498$k) and the time per step by only $1.7$. The overhead is
therefore worst on small models and amortises as the model grows --- the opposite of how a method
that explicitly inverted a curvature matrix behaves, and visible in the same table: Shampoo, which
does per-layer inverse roots at every step, costs $294\times$ gradient descent at $L=32$ against
the reference's $55\times$. At that depth the reference is the \emph{cheaper} of the two.

Momentum and isotropic noise are free on top of the reference: they add an elementwise operation
to a step already dominated by the solve, and drawing the noise itself is unmeasurable at
$0.0$\,ms. The projected arms are not free, and it is worth being exact about why, since "noise is
expensive" would be the wrong lesson. The cost is a \emph{second linear solve}, not the noise.
Measured at $L=8$: the reference step's own $50$ iterations cost $76$\,ms, or $1.52$\,ms each; the
projection's $300$ iterations cost $492$\,ms, or $1.64$\,ms each. The per-iteration cost is
essentially the same and the factor of six is entirely iteration count. Reducing the projection to
$150$ iterations halves it, at the price of leaking $3.0\%$ of the noise's operator motion back in
rather than $1.2\%$.

depth sweep's own logs, including data loading, evaluation probes, alignment probes and

\midrule
gradient descent   & $9$\,s   & $38$\,s  & $65$\,s  & $1\times$ \\
Adam               & $10$\,s  & $38$\,s  & $78$\,s  & $1.2\times$ \\
reference ($k=50$) & $111$\,s & $202$\,s & $313$\,s & $\mathbf{4.8\times}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The end-to-end ratio is $4.8\times$ where the per-step ratio is $152\times$, and both are correct.
A gradient-descent run spends $65$\,s on $12000$ steps that cost $0.5$\,ms each, so over $90\%$ of
its wall clock is evaluation, probing and I/O rather than optimisation. For the cheap arms the
optimiser is not what one is paying for. We report both because the per-step figure is the property
of the method and the end-to-end figure is the property of the experiment, and a reader deciding
whether the reference is affordable needs the second.

A final note in the method's favour that a wall-clock table cannot show: the reference has no
learning-rate grid to search. Its target size $\eta$ transfers across depth unchanged
(Section~\ref{sec:e2}), while the baselines' useful learning rates move by two to three orders of
magnitude between $L=2$ and $L=32$ and had to be swept at every depth. At equal \emph{tuning}
budget rather than equal wall clock, the comparison is considerably kinder to the reference than
the numbers above suggest.

\subsection{Cost of the diagnostics}

The alignment probe is the expensive measurement, not the optimiser. It solves the reference to
$k = 3000$ with damping, in float64, and its cost grows linearly with depth: $4.0$\,s at $L=8$,
$8.1$\,s at $L=16$, $16.5$\,s at $L=32$ per probe. At the default of one probe every $2000$ steps
this is a few percent of a run at $L=8$ and around a fifth of one at $L=32$.

At CIFAR-10 scale the same probe is not affordable in float64. The step map there has $3.07$M
equations against $40$k for MNIST-1D --- a $77\times$ increase, with every LSQR iteration growing
in proportion --- so the probe runs in float32 with batch $32$ and $k = 1200$, and the relative
normal-equation residual is recorded at every probe so that a reduced budget appears in the data as
a worse residual rather than as a silently wrong number.
